Architecture competitions have a problem. Not the idea of them — the idea is good. A brief, a prize, an open call for talent. But the execution has drifted so far from that premise that competitions now routinely extract value from the very people they claim to serve.

## Designers Pay to Compete

Entry fees of $40–100 are standard. For international or prestigious competitions, fees can exceed $200 per submission. Multiply that by a typical field of 500 entrants and an organizer collects $20,000–$100,000 in fees alone — often more than the prize pool itself.

This inverts the entire logic of a competition. The organizer is supposed to be putting up something of value to attract talent. Instead, the talent is funding the organizer.

## Prize Money Is Unverifiable

Did the winner actually get paid? When? The full amount? There's no public record. Winners occasionally post on social media; organizers self-report. But there's no mechanism for the architecture community to verify that a competition's promises were kept.

The result is a market where reputation travels slowly and fraud is hard to prove until it's already happened to you.

## IP Rights Are Buried

Read the fine print of most competition briefs. You'll find language like "organizer retains the right to use submitted designs for any purpose." Or "submission constitutes a non-exclusive, royalty-free, perpetual license."

Designers are handing over months of work — sometimes $10,000+ worth of time — without realizing they've signed away meaningful rights. The brief says "competition." The contract says something different.

## What We're Doing Differently

Arch Competitions is built around three non-negotiables:

**Free to submit, always.** No entry fees. Ever. If a competition is listed on our platform, it costs nothing to enter. Organizers fund a prize pool (minimum $100) to list — that's the only financial barrier, and it runs in the right direction.

**Transparent prize pools.** Every competition shows its prize in real time. Funds are held in escrow before the competition opens. When prizes are paid out, it's verifiable on-chain. There's no "trust us" — the money is either there or it isn't.

**IP terms that protect designers.** Our default terms are clear: designers own their work. Organizers who want broader rights have to flag it explicitly, and we surface those flags prominently so entrants know what they're agreeing to.

## The 5% Model

We take a 5% platform fee — but only when prizes are actually paid out. If a competition is cancelled or no prizes are awarded, we take nothing.

That alignment matters. We only succeed when competitions succeed. There's no incentive for us to list competitions that don't pay out, and there's no way for us to profit from entry fees that don't exist.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

A university launches a student housing competition. They fund a $5,000 prize pool — it's locked in escrow before the call opens. Designers can see the exact balance. Submissions are free. When winners are selected, payouts happen on-chain and are publicly verifiable.

That's it. No hidden fees, no rights grabs, no unverifiable promises. Just a brief, a funded prize, and a fair shot.

We think that's what competitions were supposed to be.
