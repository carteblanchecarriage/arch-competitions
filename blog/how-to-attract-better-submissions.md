Every organizer wants the same outcome: a field where the entrants they'd most want to win are actually in the room. Conventional wisdom says the way to get there is a bigger prize. That's not wrong, exactly, but it's not the lever doing most of the work. Most competitions don't pay participants beyond the prize itself, and the [Procore analysis of the format](https://www.procore.com/library/architecture-competitions) notes plainly that critics see this as organizations "getting architects to submit designs for little to no money," even as the format persists because it genuinely helps early-career practices build a portfolio and reputation. Against that trade-off, prize size alone rarely explains why an experienced practice chooses one open call over another.

What tends to explain it is risk — specifically, how much of the risk an organizer has visibly absorbed before asking anyone to commit their time. A handful of factors do most of that work, and none of them require a larger budget.

## Entry fees select for desperation, not talent

Entry fees in the $40–100 range are common enough to be treated as normal, but they don't function as a quality filter. They function as an affordability filter, and affordability correlates weakly with design ability and strongly with career stage and firm size. The design industry has a long-standing position on the underlying problem: professional bodies including [AIGA maintain an explicit stance against speculative work](https://www.aiga.org/resources/aiga-position-on-spec-work) — unpaid or underpaid creative labor performed on the chance of winning something — on the grounds that it devalues the profession and disadvantages practitioners who can least afford to work for free.

Architecture's own critics have made the same point about the competition system specifically. A [survey of industry figures published by ArchitectureAU](https://architectureau.com/articles/fifteen-thoughts-on-how-to-improve-the-architectural-competition-system/) called for competitions to be decoupled from the fee-heavy legal processes that surround them, and for concept fees to be paid when a winning project doesn't proceed — treating entrants' time as something with a cost, not a donation. Organizers who rely on entry fees to help fund the prize are usually revealing something about the brief itself: the ambition of the program exceeds the budget behind it.

## The brief as the only document that matters

The [Royal Architectural Institute of Canada's guidance on competition briefs](https://raic.org/competition-brief) and RIBA's [published rationale for running a competition](https://architecture.com/awards-and-competitions-landing-page/competitions-landing-page/why-hold-an-riba-competition) converge on the same shape: clear objectives, real site information, explicit program requirements, a stated budget, and assessment criteria disclosed before the deadline rather than inferred afterward. This convergence isn't accidental — the brief is the only document an entrant has during the design phase, and every gap in it becomes either a wrong guess or a reason to abandon the entry partway through. Length and production value in a brief are frequently mistaken for rigor. A short brief that answers the questions an entrant will actually ask consistently outperforms a longer one that doesn't.

## IP terms as a screening filter

Broad rights grabs — language granting the organizer use of "all submitted designs, for any purpose, in perpetuity" — used to go largely unread. That has changed, for reasons [covered in more detail elsewhere on this site](/blog/intellectual-property-rights-in-architecture): entrants with enough experience to have been burned once now scan for this clause before reading anything else. The same ArchitectureAU survey of industry practitioners lists intellectual property remaining with the author as one of its core recommendations for reforming the competition system, not a courtesy extended by generous organizers. Default terms that leave designers in possession of their own work, with licensing obligations attached only to the winning entry, are increasingly the baseline expectation among the entrants an organizer most wants to attract.

## Jury disclosure and published results

Two practices, both low-cost, are disproportionately correlated with repeat participation: naming the jury before the submission deadline rather than after, and publishing an actual result when the competition closes. This isn't a matter of etiquette — it's written into the profession's own rulebook. The [UIA Competition Guide](https://www.uia-architectes.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/2_UIA_competition_guide_2020.pdf) requires that jury decisions be documented and that clients publicize competition results in the appropriate media, and the UNESCO–UIA Recommendation goes further: entries — including those the jury disqualified — should be exhibited publicly, by name, for a minimum of fourteen days. Anonymity during judging and transparency afterward are treated as two halves of the same principle, not competing values.

Competitions that go quiet after the deadline are rarely concealing anything; more often the announcement simply falls off the priority list once the internal decision is made. But from outside the organization, silence is indistinguishable from a bad outcome, and entrants who watched a previous cycle go unannounced tend not to enter the next one.

> An organizer publishing winners, shortlists, and jury reasoning on a consistent basis is running a different kind of competition than one with a large prize, no visible history, and no published outcomes — and experienced entrants have learned to read the difference before they submit anything.

## Track record as a compounding asset

None of the above pays off on a first competition, because a first-time organizer has no history for entrants to evaluate against. It pays off on the second and third, when the same practices who watched how the previous cycle was handled — whether the funding held up, whether the jury was real, whether a result was ever announced — decide whether the next one is worth several weeks of their time. A documented history of funded prizes and completed judging processes becomes, over several cycles, a more reliable predictor of field quality than the prize amount stated in any single brief.
