The Town of Cape Charles, Virginia is redeveloping its former railroad and shipping harbor along the Chesapeake Bay. To help shape that future, it launched a national ideas competition in early 2026 — open to anyone 12 and older, structured across two stages, and closing submissions in September 2026. The competition was developed in coordination with The Berkley Group, the planning consultant managing the town's broader Harbor and Railyard Master Planning process.

It is a well-intentioned effort. It is also a competition with structural problems that would not pass review against any professional standard for how these events should be run.

## What the Competition Asks of Participants

Stage One requires a single 8½ x 11-inch PDF page and a 50-word abstract. Entry fees are $25 for individuals and $10 for students. Up to 25 semifinalists will be selected by a jury of regional architecture professors in October 2026. Each semifinalist receives $100 and a stipend to continue.

Stage Two runs through spring 2027. Five finalists will be selected to develop preliminary design proposals, receiving "additional financial support and access to professional design and technical assistance" — terms that are not defined anywhere in publicly available competition materials. Those five teams will present to the Town Council in summer 2027 as "test fits" for the master plan. In October 2027, Cape Charles residents will vote on a People's Choice selection.

The competition brief is explicit that outputs are not construction documents. This is an ideas competition. The Town is not obligated to implement any of the proposals.

## The Fee Math Works Against Participants

Entry fees in ideas competitions are common enough that they've become normalized. They shouldn't be.

The Town of Cape Charles is asking people to spend time — potentially substantial professional time — developing ideas that will inform a municipal master planning process. The planning process itself has a budget. The Berkley Group is a paid consultant. The competition is, in structural terms, a mechanism for crowdsourcing alternatives to the consultant's own work. Participants fund that mechanism at $25 per entry.

The prize math makes this worse. Twenty-five semifinalists at $100 each equals $2,500 in semifinalist prizes. With only a few hundred entrants — a modest number for a national open call — the entry fees collected would match or exceed that figure before a single finalist is named. This is the pattern that has made architecture competitions a loss proposition for practitioners: the people putting up value are not the organizers.

> The Town is asking people to pay $25 to help plan a harbor it will redevelop with public funds. The competition is the planning tool. The entry fee is the entrant's cost of participating in someone else's civic process.

The UIA Accord on Competitions and the AIA's guidelines for design competitions both address this asymmetry. The AIA's framework states plainly that prize money should be commensurate with the work required. The Cape Charles brief specifies a maximum submission of one page and 50 words — and still charges a fee. The format limits the work; the fee remains.

## A Jury of One Profession

The competition jury consists of "regional architecture professors." That is its only described characteristic.

A harbor redevelopment in a small coastal town raises questions that no architecture faculty alone is well-positioned to evaluate: maritime history, coastal ecology, economic development, accessible public space, the needs of fishing and boating communities, cultural heritage, and the preferences of the people who actually live there. None of these perspectives are represented in the described jury composition.

Standard competition frameworks require juries to include at least one professional registered in the relevant field — architects for architecture competitions — but also owner-side representatives and, where public interest is central to the brief, community representatives with direct knowledge of the site. The rationale is not diversity for its own sake. It is that ideas competitions succeed when the evaluation criteria reflect the full complexity of the problem, not just the discipline-specific priorities of the evaluators.

The Cape Charles competition is explicitly open to "students, architects, artists, historians, educators, and community members." It should be judged by a group whose expertise reflects that breadth. A panel of architecture professors is a reasonable component of a jury for this brief. It is not a sufficient jury on its own.

## The Berkley Group Problem

The competition was developed "in coordination with The Berkley Group," which is separately under contract to produce the Harbor and Railyard Master Plan. These two processes — the paid consultant's plan and the open ideas competition — run in parallel and are intended to inform each other.

This relationship raises a question the competition materials do not answer: who controls the brief?

If The Berkley Group shaped the competition brief, selected the jury, defined the submission criteria, and will integrate the results into the master plan it is being paid to produce, then the ideas competition is not an independent check on the consultant's assumptions. It is a legitimizing mechanism for them. The output of a competition whose terms were written by the consultant advising on the outcome is not a meaningful alternative to that consultant's work. It is a sampling of options that the consultant found worth asking about.

None of this may be true of Cape Charles. The Berkley Group may have played a limited role. The town council may have controlled the brief independently. That information is not publicly available. A well-run competition would make it available.

## What the Submission Format Signals

One page. Fifty words. These constraints are unusual even for ideas competitions, where format restrictions typically aim to equalize effort between student and professional entries, not eliminate substantive content.

The effect is that Stage One cannot produce ideas with enough depth to evaluate rigorously. A single diagram and two sentences of explanation tells the jury almost nothing about whether a concept is workable, contextually appropriate, or original. At this level of abstraction, the jury is selecting aesthetics and optimism, not ideas.

The format may be intentional — Stage Two gives semifinalists room to develop further — but it also means Stage One is functioning as a popularity contest between gut impressions, evaluated by a panel of architecture professors who have no more context than the entrants provided them.

A more robust approach: two to four pages, with a site map, a brief concept narrative, and an explicit response to the competition's stated criteria. This is still light enough to be accessible to a 12-year-old; it is substantive enough to produce ideas worth judging.

## What the Competition Does Well

The two-stage structure is appropriate for the problem. Testing a broad field before committing resources to detailed development is standard practice, and the timeline — with Stage Two running through spring 2027 — gives genuine development time to the five finalists.

The People's Choice vote is a legitimizing mechanism worth keeping. Resident participation in evaluating proposals for public space is good process, even when the vote is advisory.

The age eligibility is genuinely democratic, even if the jury composition doesn't reflect it. An ideas competition about civic space should hear from the full community. The instinct is right.

And the competition is transparent about what it is: an ideas exercise, not a procurement. The brief does not promise a commission. It does not pretend the outputs are binding. This is more than some competitions offer.

## The Standard to Measure Against

The UIA Accord on Competitions — the professional standard governing international architecture competitions — requires that competitions be open, that judging be anonymous, that prizes be meaningful relative to the effort required, that jury composition be disclosed in advance and include qualified professionals across relevant disciplines, and that results be published. The AIA's guidelines mirror these requirements for domestic competitions.

The Cape Charles competition, as publicly described, satisfies almost none of them:

- **Anonymity:** No mention of anonymized submissions during jury review.
- **Prize proportionality:** $100 for semifinalists in exchange for a $25 entry fee and substantial development work.
- **Jury composition:** One profession, no community representation, no landscape or planning expertise described.
- **Stage Two terms:** Unspecified financial support and unspecified technical assistance.
- **Results publication:** No commitment to publish non-winning submissions or finalist proposals.

This is not a damning indictment. Cape Charles is a small town running an ideas competition for a harbor redevelopment, not a major institution conducting a €300,000 international procurement. The stakes are different. But the habits that produce extractive competitions — charging participants, narrowing juries, leaving terms vague — operate at every scale. They are not a function of budget; they are a function of competition design.

## The Verdict

The Cape Charles Railroad Harbor Ideas Competition is civic engagement with a structural problem at its core. The Town wants ideas from its community and the broader profession. That impulse is worth supporting. The mechanism it chose undervalues the contribution it's asking for, narrows the evaluation criteria to a single discipline, and embeds the exercise inside a paid planning process whose relationship to the competition is not clearly defined.

A fair competition at this scale would cost nothing to enter, pay prizes that reflect the work, seat a jury that represents the brief's full range of concerns, define Stage Two terms in advance, and commit to publishing results — including non-winning proposals. None of these changes require additional budget. They require intentional design.

What Cape Charles has instead is a competition organized the way competitions have always been organized: by people who have not thought carefully about what they are asking participants to give, and in exchange for what.
